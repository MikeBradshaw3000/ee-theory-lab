# The Substrate's Trajectory: From Inverted ABM to Interacting-Particle Instrument

**Status:** Exploratory-tier working document; candidate Annex entry (TDA-004) if the Annex protocol is ratified. Drafted 2026-09-05. Purpose: the trajectory recounted here exists in the repository only atomistically — scattered across specifications, rulings, primers, and commit history. This document preserves it as prose: what was done, in what order, under what pressure, and what each turn revealed about the method's actual nature. It is methodological genealogy — it explains where the instrument came from and is not a specification of anything. Dates and technical particulars are stated to the best of the assembled record; where the repository's atomistic record conflicts with this prose, the repository governs and this document should be corrected.

---

## 1. The starting point: an inversion, present from the first day

The computational program began in April 2026, and it began with an inversion that was deliberate from the start rather than discovered along the way.

Canonical agent-based modeling in the social sciences puts the theory in the agents. Behavioral rules, dispositions, and interaction heuristics are the hypothesis; the macroscopic pattern is grown from them, and growing it is the explanatory act. Our program adopted the standard tooling of that tradition — Mesa, the Python ABM framework — and pointed it in the opposite direction. The theory lived at the top, in Landau form, before any agent existed: the two-stage cascade, the order parameters, the feedback operator. The agents were built empty on purpose. No interiority, no preferences, no decision rules — nothing but stochastic realization of participation under a locally configured Λ. This was the theory's ontological stance (action, not decision) imposed on a framework whose usual users assume the reverse. Mesa's machinery was thereby made to serve a role its tradition does not contemplate: not growing a macro pattern from hypothesized micro rules, but realizing, at agent grain, dynamics whose hypothesis lives entirely at field level.

Two consequences of that first choice shaped everything after. First, the mean-field approximation was never a summary of the agent model — it preceded and governed it. The MFA was specified, cross-validated for internal consistency, and then numerically integrated as a small pure-Python script: NumPy integrating two ODEs, sweeping Λ, producing the bifurcation diagrams — the S-curve in ρ, the pitchfork in Ψ. The working metaphor recorded at the time: the specification is the sheet music, the ODE script a synthesizer playing it to hear whether the composition sounds right, and Mesa the full orchestra to come. NumPy was therefore present at the referent level from the first week; what changed later was the orchestra. Second, because the agents carried no hypothesis, the agent-level work could only ever be a fidelity question — does the mean-field picture survive finite, local, stochastic realization? — and never a demonstration. That framing, later formalized as the MFA-fidelity program, was implicit in the inversion itself.

## 2. The Mesa period: what the framework did and did not provide

The early substrate ran in Mesa 3.x: a toroidal grid, synchronous two-phase update through the framework's step/advance idiom, the A3 baseline configuration, and the environment discipline (pinned versions, virtual-environment preflight checks, the PowerShell byte-order-mark idioms) that became the program's operational culture. Mesa provided scaffolding: agent containers, grid topology, an update loop, and a familiar vocabulary for standing the thing up quickly.

What it did not provide was anything the dynamics needed. The theory's update is synchronous: every cell advances simultaneously from the same snapshot, with draw order fixed by specification. Mesa's central service — a scheduler managing who acts when — had nothing to schedule. The framework's agent objects held no state that arrays could not hold, and its activation machinery was bypassed in substance from the beginning by the two-phase discipline. The substrate was, structurally, a stochastic lattice system wearing an ABM framework's clothes. This was not yet articulated; it was latent in the design and would be forced into the open by practical pressure.

## 3. The migration: overhead forces the issue, and the issue turns out to be identity

The pressure had two recorded grounds (substrate specification v1.1 S7; canonical executable header): Mesa 3.x API regressions, and performance — production scale (a 50×50 grid over thousands of ticks, across sweeps and seeds) was not practically runnable through the object-per-agent loop. The performance ground is structural, not incidental: with synchronous, uniform update, all heterogeneity lives in per-cell state rather than per-agent rules, so the loop over thousands of Python objects purchased nothing that whole-grid array expressions could not do in compiled operations, orders of magnitude faster. The response was reimplementation: the apparatus mathematics rewritten in vectorized NumPy, with the array program executed as the working substrate. The transitional period is visible in the repository's middle strata: battery scripts that reimplement the dynamics locally in NumPy while still importing Mesa as a pinned environment dependency and running parity checks for mathematical equivalence — byte-for-byte agreement verified before any substantive run. The migration was conservative by temperament: the old framework was held alongside until equivalence was established, then retired.

The retirement produced the program's most consequential pieces of methodology, none of which were the migration's goal:

**The equivalence clause, and its maturation into spec-canonicality.** Establishing that the NumPy program was a legal realization required saying what it was equivalent *to* — and the honest answer was not "Mesa" but "the specification." The specification defines the locked dynamics (the update steps, draw order, constants); any implementation producing them is a legal realization. What began as a migration justification matured into the program's spine: the spec, not any codebase, is the referent. This is the principle on which the planned blind rebuild rests, and it exists because the migration forced the question.

**The sequence-preserving discipline.** For two differently structured implementations to be checkable against each other at bit level, they must consume the pseudorandom stream identically — hence cell-by-cell initialization draws and one grid draw per tick, structuring the vectorized code to walk the same stream a per-cell implementation would. This discipline outlived its Mesa-facing origin: it is what makes bit-identity an achievable verification target for any future independent implementation.

**The recognition of what the method had been all along.** With the framework gone, the substrate stood revealed as what the inversion had made it from the start: a stochastic lattice simulation testing whether a field-level theory survives finite realization — interacting-particle methods in service of mean-field theory, the statistical physicist's use of simulation, not the social scientist's. The vocabulary had drifted ahead of the recognition ("substrate," not "model"); the migration caught the practice up to the vocabulary. Mesa was subsequently recorded as blocked in the substrate specification, with the scheduler-vacancy argument as grounds, and eventually retired even as an environment concern. A Mesa-versus-NumPy identical-telemetry artifact was never produced and never needed to be: equivalence is spec-defined and gate-certified, not demonstrated against a running twin.

The counterfactual, settled in later review and worth preserving with the trajectory: the experiment never needed Mesa. The dynamics could have been NumPy from the first day. But the program passed through Mesa and extracted, on the way out, the equivalence discipline, the sequence-preserving idiom, and the spec-canonicality principle — assets whose crispness owes something to the fact that a migration forced them to be stated. Mesa was a dependency of the development history, never of the dynamics.

## 4. The feedback era: complexity in the calculation, fidelity to the ontology

The most recent development stratum — the Gate B specification work and the substrate's `become_survive` machinery — added substantial computational complexity while touching the theory not at all. This distinction is the stratum's defining feature and the reason it belongs in this trajectory: it is substrate development in service of the ontology, not theoretical development wearing computational clothes.

The theory had committed the feedback operator Q from the beginning: a slow channel reading the macro state (ρ, Ψ) and writing to the individual bases, diagonal in structure, on a timescale separated from the cascade. Implementing Q's world in the substrate required machinery the earlier strata never needed — telemetry families tracking how agent populations change under terrain that is itself changing, verification gates (the Gate B apparatus, with its harness preconditions and certified-claim scoping) proportioned to the new risk surface, and rulings that bounded what the new machinery may claim (the certified equivalence claim scoped to mean equivalence with a priced gross-divergence screen; the `become_survive` family's permanent restriction to the Q-disabled subset). The calculations grew heavier; the gates grew correspondingly stricter.

What makes this ontology-accommodation rather than mere feature growth: population change in this substrate had to be modeled the way everything else is modeled — as terrain-shaped event, not agent choice. Agents do not decide to enter, persist, or exit any more than they decide to participate; becoming and surviving are realizations under structural conditions, and the machinery's design burden was precisely to carry richer lifecycle dynamics without opening any door to interiority. The added complexity is thus the cost of extending the action ontology's reach into territory — endogenously changing populations under feedback — where a conventional ABM would simply have written a decision rule and moved on. The theory sat still; the substrate grew toward it.

## 5. What the trajectory means

Read whole, the trajectory is a single movement: a method discovering its own identity under pressure. The inversion (April) made the program something other than canonical ABM from the first day, but the tooling said otherwise. Computational overhead forced a migration; the migration forced the equivalence question; the equivalence question surfaced spec-canonicality; and spec-canonicality, together with the MFA-as-referent structure, made the practice legible as what it had structurally been all along — an interacting-particle instrument for a field-level theory. The feedback era then demonstrated the mature pattern: when the theory's committed structure demands more of the substrate, the substrate gains complexity and verification machinery, and the theory gains nothing it did not already have.

Three uses follow. For the manuscript: the methods framing should not sign the ABM contract with reviewers — the design refuses behavioral realism, calibrated decision rules, and agent-parameter sensitivity studies on principle, and the correct genre claim (stochastic lattice realization tested for fidelity against a mean-field referent) both fits the practice and satisfies the simulation-methodology literature's published remedies more naturally than most work carrying the ABM label. For the program's internal language: residual "ABM" phrasings in the record are candidates for the same deliberate rename treatment as the equivalence clause. For the blind rebuild: this trajectory is the narrative form of the rebuild's premise — the spec is the referent because the program learned, by living it, that implementations are interchangeable and specifications are not.
